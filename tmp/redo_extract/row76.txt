Summary 
20 
Genomics is adopting autonomous AI agents that interpret genomes from natural-
21 
language instructions faster than it is building the means to trust them. We report the 
22 
first large-scale controlled evaluation of where, in an agentic genomic pipeline, 
23 
correctness must reside for the system to be trustworthy at clinical scale. Using 
24 
pharmacogenomics, a domain where errors are measurable and sometimes lethal, we 
25 
benchmarked nine frontier large language models across 44,550 scored evaluations on 
26 
110 pharmacogenomic cases, and tested model interpretation of real star-allele 
27 
diplotypes from more than 7,000 individuals in three ancestrally diverse populations. 
28 
Trustworthiness proved to be a property of pipeline architecture, not of the model. 
29 
Letting the model reason was stochastic and unsafe, and grounding it in the correct 
30 
guidelines by retrieval paradoxically increased lethal-class errors. Encoding the 
31 
validated decision logic as a versioned skill and executing it as code made the 
32 
pharmacogenomic mapping exact, auditable and identical across models, confining all 
33 
residual error to a single input-interpretation step. On individual genomes, unguarded 
34 
model interpretation degraded along an ancestry gradient; execution removes this 
35 
gradient from the clinical mapping, relocating it to the auditable completeness of the 
36 
input caller. This establishes a generalisable, auditable architecture for trustworthy 
37 
agentic genome interpretation at scale. 
38

52 
Significance 
53 
Genomics is adopting autonomous, language-model-mediated agents faster than it is 
54 
building the standards needed to trust them. On a pharmacogenomic benchmark with 
55 
lethal-class consequences, we show that an agent’s trustworthiness is not a property of 
56 
the model but of how the agent is constrained: correctness must be moved out of the 
57 
stochastic model into a versioned skill executed as code, with the model confined to 
58 
interpreting heterogeneous input. This gives the field a transferable architecture for 
59 
trustworthy agentic genome interpretation, a predicted route to deploying it so that every 
60 
emitted answer is correct (execute the validated skill, call the input deterministically, and 
61 
abstain on the irreducible residual), and a way to develop genomic skills as validated, 
62 
executable, versioned units rather than prompts. Following a validation framework 
63 
described elsewhere, we use clinical-grade to mean determinism, auditability, 
64 
traceability to versioned components and population-invariant performance, all achieved 
65 
under skill-constrained execution. We distinguish two senses of population 
66 
performance: the executed clinical mapping is population-invariant by construction, 
67 
verified across European, Latin American and East African origin individuals, whereas 
68 
the model’s interpretation of real, ancestrally diverse diplotypes is not, degrading along 
69 
an ancestry gradient, which is precisely why the mapping must be executed rather than 
70 
reasoned. We do not claim full clinical validation, which would additionally require non-
71 
canonical inputs, real-world genomic and clinical data, human comparators and multi-
72 
site concordance.

Introduction 
75 
Genomics has entered a phase in which AI agents can autonomously discover, 
76 
configure, execute and chain bioinformatics operations from natural-language 
77 
instructions (1). This paradigm, agentic genomics, shifts the bottleneck in computational 
78 
biology from building pipelines to validating them (1), and with it comes a new class of 
79 
risk: agents can generate results faster than researchers can check them, and the most 
80 
dangerous errors are the ones that look plausible but are wrong. A companion 
81 
Perspective to this paper (1) sets out a tiered validation framework for agentic genomics 
82 
and identifies the clinical-grade tier as the most demanding, requiring outputs that are 
83 
deterministic, auditable, traceable to specific versioned components, and population-
84 
invariant. Here we operationalise that framework on a single clinical-task substrate, 
85 
pharmacogenomic interpretation, to ask the question that decides whether agentic 
86 
genome interpretation can be automated at scale: where in an agentic pipeline must 
87 
correctness reside for the system to be trustworthy? 
88 
 
89 
Two answers dominate practice. The first is to let the model reason from its training 
90 
prior. The second, retrieval-augmented generation, grounds the model in retrieved 
91 
evidence and lets it reason over that (2, 3, 4); large language models encode 
92 
substantial clinical knowledge (5), and the reflex in biology is to surface the relevant 
93 
guidelines and let the model apply them. This paper tests a third, structural answer:

95 
framework, a skill is a self-contained, versioned unit of bioinformatics functionality 
96 
whose declarative specification (SKILL.md) defines the analytical logic, expected inputs 
97 
and outputs, and domain-expert decisions (1, 6). A skill can be used in two ways: the 
98

99

100

101 
 
102 
Pharmacogenomics is a rigorous test domain: the genotype-to-phenotype-to-
103 
recommendation mapping is well-defined by the Clinical Pharmacogenetics 
104 
Implementation Consortium (CPIC) Level A guideline corpus (7), errors are clinically 
105 
measurable, and the task demands precise adherence to published guidance. The 
106 
benchmark includes high-stakes drug-gene pairs with documented lethal 
107 
consequences: DPYD variants where standard fluoropyrimidine dosing causes severe 
108 
or fatal toxicity in approximately 0.1 to 0.5% of carriers (8); TPMT and NUDT15 
109 
deficiencies where standard thiopurine dosing causes lethal myelosuppression (9); 
110 
CYP2C19 poor metabolisers where standard clopidogrel dosing fails to prevent stent 
111 
thrombosis (10); and HLA risk alleles where exposure to specific drugs causes Stevens-
112 
Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis or hypersensitivity reaction (11, 12). 
113 
 
114 
Concurrent work applies agents to genomic tasks but does not address this question. 
115 
Recent systems generate pharmacogenomic recommendations from the literature 
116 
through an agentic pipeline (13), or prioritise rare-disease variants for diagnosis at scale 
117 
(14); both build end-to-end pipelines and report task accuracy. Neither isolates where in 
118 
the pipeline correctness must reside, neither separates a model that reasons over a skill 
119 
from one that executes it as code, and neither establishes determinism, auditability and 
120 
model-invariance as properties of execution rather than of the model. That controlled 
121 
dissociation, not the construction of another agentic system, is our contribution. 
122 
 
123 
We benchmark nine frontier large language models along a five-step constraint gradient 
124 
that progressively relocates correctness from the model into an executed skill: (i) free-
125 
prompted; (ii) retrieval-augmented from the CPIC corpus; (iii) skill-reasoning, in which 
126 
the model applies a versioned skill’s rules and generates the answer; (iv) skill-
127 
execution, in which the model supplies a structured input and the validated skill 
128 
computes the answer in code; and (v) an answer-supplied positive control. The gradient 
129 
shows that trustworthiness is a property of where correctness sits, not of the model: the 
130 
unconstrained and retrieval-grounded conditions are stochastic and unsafe; structured 
131 
skills make capable models reliable and near model-invariant; execution makes the 
132 
clinical mapping exact and confines all residual error to one auditable step; and a 
133 
validated deterministic caller for that step closes the pipeline toward complete 
134 
correctness among emitted in-scope answers. We frame the contribution as an 
135 
architecture and an evaluation for trustworthy agentic genome interpretation at scale, 
136 
not as a claim that models become clinically correct in isolation. Figure 1 summarises 
137 
the study design.

Results 
154 
A constraint-gradient benchmark of agentic pharmacogenomic interpretation 
155 
We benchmarked nine frontier large language models (Claude Opus 4, Claude Sonnet 
156 
4, GPT-5.2, GPT-4.1, o3, o4-mini, Gemini 2.5 Flash, DeepSeek V3, Mistral Large 2) on 
157 
110 CPIC Level A cases spanning 21 markers and 35 gene-drug pairs. The three core 
158 
conditions (free-prompted, retrieval-augmented, answer-supplied) were evaluated 
159 
across three population contexts and three replicates, producing 26,730 evaluations. 
160 
The two skill conditions (skill-reasoning, skill-execution) were run on all nine models 
161 
across the same three-population, three-replicate grid, producing a further 17,820 
162 
evaluations; Mistral Large 2 is excluded from the reported skill-arm aggregate as a rate-
163 
limit artefact (it returned usable output on only 3.7% of skill-arm cells; Methods), so the 
164 
headline skill figures are over eight models, and the all-nine-model aggregate is 
165 
reported in the supplement. On the common set of eight models for which all five 
166 
conditions yield valid output (Mistral fails only under the skill prompts), the constraint 
167 
gradient is unchanged: free-prompted 79.6%, retrieval-augmented 89.1%, skill-
168 
reasoning 95.5%, skill-execution 93.3% and the answer-supplied control 100%. Outputs 
169 
were scored on phenotype identification (A1), drug-specific recommendation (A2) and 
170 
lethal-class safety action (A3) by a rigorous rescorer with a justified clinical-equivalence 
171 
layer (Methods).
